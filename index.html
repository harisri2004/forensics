Forensic accounting review · FY2021/22 – FY2025/26

Was Burberry's recovery real?

Burberry lost money, then reported a sharp recovery. I set out to prove that the recovery was an accounting effect rather than a real one. It held up.

What I was testing

Between 2022 and 2025 Burberry's revenue fell 13% and it went from £511m of profit before tax to a £66m loss. Measured from its FY2023 peak, revenue is down 22%. One year later it reported margins up, free cash flow more than doubled, and leverage down from 2.3x to 1.6x. The Remuneration Committee credited executives for the cash improvement.

A recovery that fast is worth checking, because there are legitimate accounting choices that flatter one year at the expense of another: releasing provisions taken earlier, delaying spending, or moving costs into a category excluded from headline figures. I wrote eight specific hypotheses of that kind and tried to prove each one.

The finding that came closest

Burberry spent far less on its stores than those assets were wearing out

Cutting investment raises cash flow immediately, so it is the obvious way to make a year look better than it is. Investment fell to 73% of depreciation — the lowest in the peer group, having been the highest four years earlier. Every other company in the set stayed above 100% throughout.

It does not hold up as manipulation. Burberry told the market a year in advance that it would spend less — £21m of the £38m reduction was already in guidance. The cut shows up in committed spending, not just in cash timing. And next year's guidance of £120m shows no catch-up. The company was closing stores while its peers were opening them.

What the peer data settled

The divergence from peers is real. The explanation is company-specific investment timing, not a manipulation of the reported result.

Peer comparison

Burberry holds the least inventory in the peer set, in every year

If a company were hiding obsolete stock rather than writing it down, inventory would build. Burberry's did the opposite: 188 days at FY2026 against a peer range of 204 to 344, and it sits at the 17th percentile across the whole period. This was the clearest result the peer data produced, and it ran against the direction I expected.

Method

Every claim is labelled fact, derivation, inference or unresolved, and every material figure carries a page reference to the annual report it came from. Assurance coverage was mapped per test against EY, the Audit Committee, Group Internal Audit and the FRC.

I then built a 23-metric screening model across Kering, Richemont, Moncler, Prada and Hugo Boss over the same five years, so each Burberry number could be judged against the same number at five comparable companies rather than in isolation.

How it was built: the reports were converted to searchable text so passages could be located and read against each other, and the model was built in Excel with every input traced to a page reference. The reasoning, the hypotheses and the verdicts are mine; the extraction and the arithmetic were automated so the time went into judgement rather than transcription.

Errors and corrections

I misread an inventory provision balance as a big-bath charge when the underlying charge had actually fallen. I overstated one exposure threefold. I left internal audit out of seven assurance maps. Every one of those errors made Burberry look worse than its disclosures support — a directional bias in my own method, recorded as such in the final report.

Two defects in the peer model were caught the same way: a percentile formula that was ranking Burberry against itself, and a group-assignment bug that put eleven metrics in the wrong weight class.

What this work cannot do

It uses only published disclosures. That is enough to test whether a disclosed pattern fits a suspected mechanism. It cannot judge whether a particular estimate was the best one available, and it gives no sight of transactions, models or working papers.

Conclusion

The recovery is supported by the accounting rather than produced by it. Where Burberry looks unusual against its peers, the reason is disclosed rather than buried: it told the market a year in advance that it would spend less, and volunteered that part of the reduction was timing.

I would not extrapolate the cash flow yet. Half the improvement came from spending less on stores and equipment, and up to £17m of that was deferred rather than saved. The open item is the £37m UK element of an £87m loss-based deferred tax asset, whose recovery needs roughly £300m of future UK taxable profit. On this evidence I would treat FY2026 as a real result but not yet a repeatable one, and would want the FY2026/27 outturn before reading it as a trend.
